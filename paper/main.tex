\begin{abstract}
%% DRAFT -- placeholder numbers below must be re-checked against
%% PROJECT_STATUS.md immediately before submission; written for review now.
We train a physics-informed neural operator (Transolver) to predict the
displacement field of hyperelastic solids under randomly sampled material
and loading fields, without any labeled displacement data: the network is
trained by directly minimizing the discrete total potential energy of the
body (a Deep Energy Method), following the PFEM pretraining methodology.
Two 2D benchmark geometries (a unit square under top-edge traction, and a
quarter ring under internal pressure), each combined with three isotropic
hyperelastic strain-energy densities (Neo-Hookean, Mooney--Rivlin,
Arruda--Boyce), are trained to completion, evaluated against an independent
finite-element reference, and characterized across mesh-resolution
invariance, out-of-distribution robustness, and break-even cost against both
a native and a GPU-native finite-element solver. The study is then extended
to a third, more demanding 3D benchmark: a rocking elastomeric rubber-mount
bushing with a stress-concentrating groove. The operator reproduces
displacement, strain energy, and reaction moment accurately (combined
displacement error 1.9\%, energy error 0.3\%, reaction-moment error 2.7\%),
and, judged against the cheapest finite-element mesh that matches its own
accuracy, remains 589--800$\times$ faster per sample while repaying its
one-off training cost within fewer than 1{,}900 solves. The one quantity
that does not reach the same standard is the regional Cauchy-stress field
near the groove: an input-normalization fix first resolves a training
instability, after which the regional-stress error is traced, in part, to
an under-resolved background numerical integration rather than the
network's own capacity, and a local integration refinement -- applied to
only the few mesh elements touching the region of interest, independently
of the operator's own discretization -- roughly halves the error (from
62\% to 30\%). The remaining gap is reported as an open limitation, with
several concrete directions (stress/equilibrium-aware physics losses,
mixed displacement--stress formulations, local/hierarchical representations)
identified for future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Classical finite-element (FEM) solvers for nonlinear elasticity re-solve a
new nonlinear system from scratch for every new combination of geometry,
material field, and loading, each solve requiring a full Newton--Raphson
iteration to convergence. Operator learning instead trains a single neural
network to approximate the map from problem data (material field, loading
field, geometry) directly to the solution field, so that new instances are
evaluated with a single forward pass~\cite{lu2021deeponet,li2021fno,kovachki2023neuraloperator}.
Physics-informed variants of this idea remove the need for expensive
labeled training data entirely, by training the network to satisfy the
governing equations or minimize a governing energy functional directly,
rather than to regress against precomputed solutions~\cite{raissi2019pinn,e2018deepritz}.

This work follows the PFEM (\emph{Pretrain Finite Element Method})
approach~\cite{wang2026pfem}: a Transolver-architecture
operator~\cite{wu2024transolver}, built on the attention
mechanism~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, is trained without any labeled
displacement data, by directly minimizing the discretized total potential
energy of the elastic body -- a Deep Energy Method (DEM)
objective~\cite{samaniego2020energy,nguyen2020deep}, closely related to
the Deep Ritz method~\cite{e2018deepritz} and to the variational
physics-informed operator framework~\cite{eshaghi2025vino}, rather than a
supervised regression loss against finite-element solutions. The same
underlying idea -- minimizing an energy functional with a neural ansatz,
instead of imposing the strong-form PDE residual as in standard PINNs --
has previously been developed for finite-deformation
hyperelasticity~\cite{nguyen2020deep,abueidda2022dlem}, strain-gradient
elasticity~\cite{nguyen2021parametric}, contact
problems~\cite{bai2025energy}, and fracture~\cite{goswami2020transfer,wang2026xdem,zhang2026meshfree},
predominantly at the level of a single geometry/material instance rather
than as a trained operator generalizing across a distribution of material
and loading fields.

The present study covers two 2D benchmark geometries -- a unit square
under top-edge traction (B1) and a quarter ring under internal pressure
(B2) -- combined with three hyperelastic strain-energy densities
(Neo-Hookean~\cite{rivlin1948large}, Mooney--Rivlin~\cite{mooney1940theory},
Arruda--Boyce~\cite{arruda1993threedim}), for six total (geometry,
material) cases, and a third, substantially more demanding 3D benchmark: a
rocking elastomeric rubber-mount bushing with a stress-concentrating
groove in its rigid inner core (B3). An independent Newton--Raphson
Total-Lagrangian finite-element solver, built on top of
torch-fem~\cite{meyer2024torchfem} and
PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch,baydin2018automatic}, generates
ground-truth samples used only for validation, never for training.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:governing}
states the Total Lagrangian hyperelastic potential-energy formulation and the
three constitutive models used. Section~\ref{sec:geometries} defines the three
benchmark geometries. Section~\ref{sec:discretization} describes the spatial
discretization and the finite-element reference used for validation.
Section~\ref{sec:architecture} describes the Transolver architecture and the
Deep Energy Method training objective. Section~\ref{sec:results-2d} reports
results for the six 2D (geometry, material) cases, and
Section~\ref{sec:results-3d} extends the study to the 3D rocking-bushing
benchmark. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the findings and open
limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Governing Equations and Material Models}
\label{sec:governing}

\subsection{Total Lagrangian hyperelasticity}
\label{sec:total-lagrangian}

Let $\Omega_0 \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ ($d=2$ for B1/B2, $d=3$ for B3) denote the
known, undeformed reference configuration of the body, and let
$\mathbf{u}:\Omega_0 \to \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the displacement field. The
deformation gradient and its Jacobian are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I} + \nabla_0 \mathbf{u}, \qquad J = \det \mathbf{F}.
\end{equation}
For a hyperelastic material with strain-energy density $\psi(\mathbf{F})$, the
total potential energy of the body under a prescribed traction (or pressure)
$\mathbf{t}$ on the loaded boundary $\Gamma_t \subset \partial\Omega_0$ is
\begin{equation}
  \Pi[\mathbf{u}] = \underbrace{\int_{\Omega_0} \psi(\mathbf{F})\,\mathrm{d}V}_{U[\mathbf{u}]}
  \;-\; \underbrace{\int_{\Gamma_t} \mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{u}\,\mathrm{d}A}_{W[\mathbf{u}]}.
  \label{eq:potential-energy}
\end{equation}
The equilibrium displacement field minimizes the total potential energy,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{u}^\ast = \arg\min_{\mathbf{u}} \Pi[\mathbf{u}],
  \label{eq:min-energy}
\end{equation}
which is the objective optimized directly by the neural operator
(Section~\ref{sec:architecture}); no labeled displacement data are required,
only pointwise evaluation of $\psi$ and of the prescribed boundary traction.
For B3, the applied load is a prescribed rigid-body rotation of a rigid inner
core rather than a traction, so $W \equiv 0$ and $\Pi = U$ (Section~\ref{sec:geometries}).

For numerical integration, the body is discretized into elements (bilinear
quadrilaterals, Q4, for B1/B2; trilinear hexahedra, Hexa1, for B3) with full
Gauss quadrature. The internal energy is approximated as
\begin{equation}
  U_h(\mathbf{u}) = \sum_{e} \sum_{g} \psi(\mathbf{F}_g)\, w_g \, \det \mathbf{J}_g,
  \label{eq:discrete-energy}
\end{equation}
where $g$ indexes the Gauss points of element $e$, $w_g$ are the quadrature
weights, and the shape-function gradients used to assemble $\mathbf{F}_g$
depend only on the fixed reference-element geometry; they are computed once
per mesh and reused for every sample and every training iteration.

\subsection{Neo-Hookean (compressible)}
With the first invariant $I_1 = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{F}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{F})$,
the compressible Neo-Hookean strain-energy density is
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{\text{NH}} = \frac{\mu}{2}(I_1 - d) - \mu \ln J + \frac{\lambda}{2}(\ln J)^2,
  \label{eq:neo-hookean}
\end{equation}
which yields the first Piola--Kirchhoff stress
$\mathbf{P} = \partial\psi/\partial\mathbf{F} = \mu(\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf T}) + \lambda(\ln J)\mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf T}$.
This expression, and the corresponding consistent material tangent, were
derived analytically and used in the Newton--Raphson solver that generates
the reference finite-element solutions.

\subsection{Mooney--Rivlin (2D, compressible)}
In two dimensions the second invariant $I_2 = \tfrac12\big[(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{C})^2 - \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}^2)\big]$
(with $\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{F}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{F}$) satisfies the identity
$I_2 \equiv J^2$, a consequence of the Cayley--Hamilton theorem for
$2\times 2$ matrices; the conventional three-dimensional Mooney--Rivlin form,
which treats $I_2$ as an independent invariant with reference value $3$, is
therefore not applicable in 2D. The compressible Mooney--Rivlin density used
here is instead
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{\text{MR}} = c_1(I_1-d) + c_2(J^2-1) - 2(c_1+c_2)\ln J + \frac{\lambda}{2}(\ln J)^2,
  \label{eq:mooney-rivlin}
\end{equation}
with the split between $c_1$ and $c_2$ fixed at a $3{:}1$ ratio of the
linearized shear modulus ($c_1 = \tfrac34\mu$, $c_2=\tfrac14\mu$) for every
sample.

\subsection{Arruda--Boyce (8-chain, compressible)}
With the modified (isochoric) first invariant $\bar I_1 = J^{-2/d} I_1$, the
Arruda--Boyce density is approximated by the standard five-term Pad\'e series
of the 8-chain model,
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{\text{AB}} = \mu \sum_{i=1}^{5} \frac{c_i}{\lambda_m^{2i-2}}\big(\bar I_1^{\,i} - d^{\,i}\big)
  + \frac{\lambda}{2}(\ln J)^2,
  \label{eq:arruda-boyce}
\end{equation}
with $(c_1,\dots,c_5) = (\tfrac12,\tfrac{1}{20},\tfrac{11}{1050},\tfrac{19}{7000},\tfrac{519}{673750})$
and the chain-locking stretch $\lambda_m$ fixed for every sample in this
study (not sampled per instance).

For all three constitutive models the first Piola--Kirchhoff stress is
$\mathbf{P} = \partial\psi/\partial\mathbf{F}$ and the consistent material
tangent is $\mathbb{C} = \partial^2\psi/\partial\mathbf{F}\partial\mathbf{F}$,
used only inside the reference Newton--Raphson solver. For Neo-Hookean, both
were derived analytically; for Mooney--Rivlin and Arruda--Boyce they were
obtained by automatic differentiation (\texttt{jax.grad}, \texttt{jax.jacfwd}),
avoiding manual derivation of the more complex tangent operators. All three
models are calibrated from the same sampled Young's modulus $E$ and
Poisson's ratio $\nu$ fields via the standard isotropic linear-elasticity
relations $\mu = E/[2(1+\nu)]$, $\lambda = E\nu/[(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)]$; this
reproduces the target linear-elastic moduli exactly for Neo-Hookean, gives an
effective shear modulus of $1.5\mu$ for the $3{:}1$ Mooney--Rivlin split, and
reproduces the target shear modulus to within a few percent for Arruda--Boyce
(a known consequence of the truncated Pad\'e series) -- all three are
nonetheless calibrated from identical $(E,\nu)$ realizations and directly
comparable across materials.

\section{Benchmark Geometries}
\label{sec:geometries}

\subsection{B1: unit square, top-edge traction}
The reference domain is the unit square $\Omega_0=(0,1)^2$. The bottom edge
is fully fixed ($\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$ on $y=0$); a traction field,
sampled independently per realization as a 1D Gaussian random field
(mean $-5.0$, standard deviation $2.0$, correlation length $0.5L_x$), is
applied along the top edge $y=1$; the two vertical edges are traction-free.
The spatial fields of Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio are independent 2D
Gaussian random fields with mean/standard deviation $(1000,200)$ for $E$ and
$(0.3,0.05)$ for $\nu$ (clipped to $[0.2,0.4]$), correlation length $0.5L_x$
in both cases, so each sample realizes a unique heterogeneous material.

\subsection{B2: quarter ring, internal pressure}
The reference domain is a quarter annulus, $R_{\text{in}}=1$, $R_{\text{out}}=2$,
$\theta\in[0,\pi/2]$. A pressure field, acting normal to the reference
(undeformed) inner boundary, is sampled as a Gaussian random field (mean
$5.0$, standard deviation $2.0$, correlation length $0.5\theta_{\max}$) along
the inner arc $r=R_{\text{in}}$; the outer arc is traction-free; symmetry
boundary conditions are imposed on the two radial edges. $E$ and $\nu$ use
the same sampling distributions as B1.

\subsection{B3: rocking rubber-mount bushing with a stress-concentrating groove}
\label{sec:b3-geometry}
The third, 3D benchmark is an elastomeric rubber-mount bushing: a hollow
rubber annulus ($R_{\text{in}}=0.5$, $R_{\text{out}}=1.0$, height $L_z=1.0$)
bonded to a rigid outer housing (fixed) and a rigid inner core given a
prescribed rigid-body rocking rotation (a true coupled $u_x$--$u_z$ rotation,
not a linear small-angle approximation). The core is bonded to the rubber
continuously over its full height, with no free/bonded boundary-condition
transition anywhere on the inner surface -- an earlier design with a
fillet-terminated bond was rejected because the bonded-to-free transition
itself reproduces a free-edge stress singularity independent of the
surrounding geometry's smoothness. The one explicit, disclosed
stress-concentrating feature is a smooth ($C^1$-continuous, raised-cosine)
circumferential groove in the core's radius profile at mid-height, with
depth $0.20$ and half-width $0.15$ (giving a closed-form radius of curvature
$\rho = 2w^2/(d\pi^2) \approx 0.0228$). As for B1/B2, $E(\theta,r,z)$ and
$\nu(\theta,r,z)$ are independent 3D Gaussian random fields (mean/standard
deviation $(1000,200)$ and $(0.45,0.02)$ respectively); the load varies
through the core's rocking angle $\phi$, sampled as a single scalar per
sample (mean $0.0504$, standard deviation $0.0184$) rather than a spatial
field, since the load here is a rigid-body rotation amplitude rather than a
distributed pressure. The geometry itself (groove depth/half-width,
$R_{\text{in}}$, $R_{\text{out}}$, $L_z$) is held fixed across every sample,
as in B1/B2.

\section{Spatial Discretization and Dataset Generation}
\label{sec:discretization}

B1 and B2 are discretized with a structured $21\times21$ mesh of Q4
elements (441 nodes, 400 elements, 1,600 Gauss points per sample); for B2
the mesh is generated in polar coordinates and mapped to Cartesian space,
preserving the structured Q4 topology. B3 is discretized with a structured
$21\times20\times19$ mesh of trilinear hexahedra (6,840 elements, 7,980
nodes). An independent Newton--Raphson Total-Lagrangian finite-element
solver (built on torch-fem~\cite{meyer2024torchfem}) generates the
ground-truth samples used only for validation.

Because the neural operator and its finite-element reference are compared on
the same discretization, agreement between the two demonstrates only that
the operator has learned that particular discretization; it says nothing
about whether the discretization itself is fine enough to represent the
underlying continuum problem. This question was addressed directly, for
B1/B2, with a mesh-convergence study of the reference solver alone (fixed,
closed-form analytical material/loading fields evaluable at any resolution,
independent of the random-field sampler used elsewhere, which is itself
parameterized by resolution). Across all six (geometry, material)
combinations, the relative change in peak displacement and total strain
energy between successive refinements is already below $0.2\%$ per step at
the $21\times21$ operating mesh, and below $0.02\%$ by $N{=}51$ -- the
reference solver is solidly in its asymptotic convergence regime at the
resolution used throughout this study.

A deeper check, against a genuine
$\sim$$10^7$-DOF numerical reference (B1$\times$Neo-Hookean, Q4, $N{=}2236$)
rather than an analytical field, confirmed an $L^2$ displacement error below
the requested $10^{-4}$ target already at $N{=}201$, while the $H^1$
semi-norm and the tangent-energy norm (induced by the reference solution's
own tangent stiffness) converge more slowly and do not reach that target
even at $N{=}1401$ -- consistent with the lower fitted convergence rates for
derivative-based norms (least-squares rates $L^2$: $1.57$, $H^1$: $0.73$,
energy: $0.86$) than for the displacement field itself. A parallel
biquadratic (Q9) reference at the same resolution converges faster in every
norm, as expected from finite-element theory, and the two element orders'
own fine solutions agree to within $2\times10^{-6}$ in $L^2$ (though not
fully to the requested $10^{-5}$ criterion in $H^1$/energy, attributed to
residual discretization error in the Q4 reference relative to Q9's faster
convergence).

\section{Neural Operator Architecture and Training Objective}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Transolver}
The neural operator used throughout is Transolver~\cite{wu2024transolver}, a
physics-attention transformer for learning solution operators on irregular
meshes. For each mesh node $i$, the input is the reference coordinate
$\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ together with a material/loading feature
vector $\mathbf{f}_i$ (the local $(E,\nu)$ pair, plus the scalar loading
value for B1/B2 or the scalar rocking angle $\phi$, broadcast to every node,
for B3); the network predicts the corresponding nodal displacement
$\mathbf{u}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{f}_i)$, with $\theta$ its trainable
parameters. The same architecture and hyperparameter configuration are used
for both B1 and B2; B3 uses the same architecture with $d{=}3$ input/output
dimensions and a correspondingly reworked feature dimension.

\subsection{Physics-informed training objective (Deep Energy Method)}
The network is trained by directly minimizing the discretized total
potential energy of Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-energy},
\begin{equation}
  \theta^\ast = \arg\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(E,\nu,\mathbf{t})\sim\mathcal{D}}
  \big[\, \Pi_h\big(\mathbf{u}_\theta(\,\cdot\,;E,\nu,\mathbf{t})\big) \,\big],
  \label{eq:dem-objective}
\end{equation}
over the sampling distribution $\mathcal{D}$ of material and loading fields
(Section~\ref{sec:geometries}); no labeled displacement data are used during
optimization. Dirichlet boundary conditions are enforced as a hard
constraint via an output transformation: the raw network output is
multiplied by a smooth ramp function that vanishes on the constrained
boundary and approaches unity away from it, so the prescribed displacement
is satisfied exactly everywhere with no penalty term or tunable penalty
weight. Because every sample within a given benchmark case shares an
identical mesh, the reference-configuration quantities entering
Eq.~\eqref{eq:discrete-energy} (the Jacobian determinant and shape-function
gradients at each Gauss point) are computed once per batch and broadcast to
every sample in it, combined with a fully parallel (not gradient-accumulated)
forward pass over the batch.

\section{Results: B1/B2 (2D Benchmarks)}
\label{sec:results-2d}

\subsection{Training protocol and final accuracy}
Before training all benchmark cases, a systematic screening study was
performed on representative case B1 with the Neo-Hookean material model
to determine a common training protocol. Candidate batch sizes
(originally 4, 8, 16; extended to 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, as real
parallel mini-batches rather than gradient accumulation) were evaluated
using short training runs with frequent validation (a fixed 400-epoch
budget per batch size, validation every 25 epochs), compared in terms of
validation accuracy, computational time, GPU memory usage, and optimizer
steps; the best-performing configuration (batch size 8) was then adopted
unchanged for all six benchmark cases.
Figure~\ref{fig:batch-screening} shows the full screened comparison,
plotted against optimizer steps, total processed samples, and wall-clock
time. The selected configuration is continued from the best checkpoint
obtained during screening, rather than retrained from scratch: training
proceeds to a maximum of 2000 epochs with early stopping enabled
(terminating if validation error fails to improve by at least $10^{-4}$
over eight consecutive validation events, 200 epochs); this continuation
also serves as the final training run for the representative case. The
training protocol established this way is subsequently applied without
modification to the remaining five cases, with no case-specific
hyperparameter tuning. All six cases completed training under this
identical protocol, each terminating by early stopping (no case reached
the 2000-epoch upper bound; Table~\ref{tab:final-results}).

Every validation/test-set error reported in this study is a per-sample,
per-displacement-component relative error, defined for the $u$-component
as $e_u = \|u_{\text{pred}}-u_{\text{exact}}\|/(\|u_{\text{exact}}\|+\epsilon)$
and identically for $v$, where $\|v\| := (\tfrac1N\sum_i v_i^2)^{1/2}$
denotes the root-mean-square of a nodal quantity over the $N$ mesh nodes
of that sample and $\epsilon=10^{-12}$ avoids division by zero. The
reported error at each epoch or table entry is the sample-average of
$0.5(e_u+e_v)$ over every sample in the reported set -- a discrete,
per-node RMS relative error, unweighted over nodes rather than a
quadrature- or Jacobian-weighted domain integral, though the two coincide
in the limit of a uniform node distribution that both B1 and B2's
structured Q4 meshes closely approximate at the resolution used
throughout this study.

\subsection{Training cost, native-FEM comparison, and break-even}
Two complementary training-cost metrics are reported, both derived from
the total training wall-clock time up to the final epoch determined by
early stopping: the average cost of processing one training sample during
a single epoch (forward pass, backward pass, and optimizer update
included), and the total training cost allocated to one sample over the
entire training process, $\text{train}_s$ -- the metric directly
comparable to that case's own native finite-element cost, since it
represents the complete one-time computational investment required to
train the operator before it can perform inference on new samples at
negligible additional cost. A third metric, inference latency, is measured
independently with a dedicated, standalone benchmark: 200 repeated forward
passes of the trained network (batch size 1, no gradient computation)
through each case's own best checkpoint, on the same GPU -- what a

\subsection{GPU-native finite-element solver: scaling and comparison to established libraries}
\label{sec:gpu-fem}
To check whether the break-even figures above depend on the finite-element
side being a slow, single-threaded CPU baseline, the GPU-native,
batched Newton--Raphson solver of Section~\ref{sec:results-2d} was also
scaled up on its own terms, and compared directly against two established
GPU finite-element codes. The solver forms its tangent densely at the
study's own mesh, which is appropriate at $N{=}21$ but cannot scale (a
dense $2$-million-by-$2$-million tangent in double precision is
$\sim\!32$~TB); a matrix-free variant, with a Jacobi-preconditioned
conjugate-gradient inner loop, was therefore used for the scaling study,
on B1$\times$Neo-Hookean, across eight resolutions up to $N{=}1401$
($3{,}925{,}602$~DOF) on a single A100.

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/image21.png}
\caption{GPU-native matrix-free solver scaling: wall-clock time and CG
  iteration count vs.\ problem size.}
\label{fig:21}
\end{figure}

The solver reaches $3{,}925{,}602$~DOF in $11.0$~hours, using only
$3{,}280$~MB of the card's 80~GB ($\sim$4\%) -- memory is not the limiting
resource at these sizes. Cost per degree of freedom is non-monotone: it
falls $6.0$-fold from $N{=}101$ to a minimum near $N{=}501$, then rises
$3.1$-fold by $N{=}1401$. The solver's own iteration counters explain the
shape: Jacobi-preconditioned CG needs $5.011\times N$ iterations per Newton
step when it converges (confirmed to within $0.6\%$ at three resolutions
where it does, $N{=}101,201,301$), but at $N\!\geq\!701$ every CG solve
hits a $2{,}000$-iteration cap without reaching tolerance, so the reported
times at those resolutions understate the converged cost; raising the cap
to $8{,}000$ confirms the $O(N)$ iteration law continues to hold ($2{,}520.8$
and $3{,}528.1$ measured vs.\ $2{,}511$ and $3{,}513$ predicted, within
$0.4\%$, at $N{=}701,1001$). A geometric multigrid preconditioner removes
the truncation entirely -- CG converges within the original $2{,}000$-iteration
budget at every resolution from $N{=}401$ to $1401$ -- and needs far fewer
iterations than Jacobi-preconditioned CG would at convergence ($318.9$
vs.\ $\sim$$7{,}020$ predicted at $N{=}1401$, a $22\times$ reduction). A
linear memory model (fixed overhead plus a per-DOF term), fitted on four
smaller resolutions before the largest case had run, predicted the
$N{=}1401$ peak memory to within $2.4\%$ out of sample.

This gap is not read here as a defect to be engineered away. Two
architectural families are being compared, not two implementations of the
same one: torch-fem always explicitly assembles a sparse tangent
stiffness matrix once per Newton iteration and solves that system
iteratively or directly, while this project's own solver never forms that
matrix at all, obtaining every Hessian-vector product it needs by
automatic differentiation instead -- the design this project's
multi-million-DOF references depend on, since an explicitly assembled
tangent at that scale would not fit in a single GPU's memory. The honest
reading is that this project's matrix-free solver trades wall-clock speed
at the sizes tested here for the ability to reach substantially larger
problems than an explicitly-assembled approach can hold in GPU memory at
all -- both properties worth stating plainly, not one to the exclusion of
the other.

\subsection{Out-of-distribution generalization}
To check how each network behaves outside its training distribution, a
separate OOD validation set of 200 samples per case was generated with
both material stiffness and loading magnitude shifted well away from the
training distribution: mean Young's modulus raised from 1000 to 1500 (a
$2.5\sigma$ shift), B1's mean top-edge traction magnitude raised from 5.0
to 9.0, and B2's mean internal pressure raised from 5.0 to 9.0 (both
roughly a $2\sigma$ shift). Each case's own existing checkpoint is
evaluated, unchanged, on both its in-distribution held-out validation set
and this new OOD set; no retraining or fine-tuning is performed. With both
columns evaluated consistently on the corrected (loss-normalized) B2
checkpoints, B1 and B2 show broadly comparable sensitivity to this shift:
degradation factors of $4.11$--$4.61\times$ for B1 and $3.94$--$5.58\times$
for B2 -- no geometry is meaningfully more or less robust than the other
under this test.

Table 11's combined shift cannot say which of the two factors -- material
or loading -- is responsible, nor whether the damage appears gradually or
at a threshold. Each shift was therefore separated and swept independently
in half-sigma steps, $k=0$ to $k=3$, on B1$\times$Neo-Hookean. The
separation is unambiguous: shifting the loading produces no measurable
degradation anywhere in the sweep (the error stays between $0.99\times$
and $1.07\times$ of its in-distribution value across the full three-sigma
range, sample noise rather than a trend), while shifting the material
carries all of it, from $1.63\times$ at half a sigma to $5.90\times$ at
three. The damage accumulates smoothly -- degradation increments per half
sigma are $0.77,0.80,0.83,0.90,0.98$, monotonically increasing, with no
threshold and no cliff -- so the operator has no stated domain of
validity, only a rate at which it degrades outside the one it was trained
on. The same isolation, run for the remaining five geometry--material
combinations at the $k{=}3\sigma$ endpoint, shows the pattern holding
without exception: shifting the loading magnitude alone produces at most
mild degradation ($0.81$--$1.35\times$), while shifting the material
stiffness alone is the dominant driver in every case ($2.27$--$5.90\times$),
an order of magnitude worse than the corresponding loading shift
throughout; the two shifts do not compound, with the combined column
below the material-only column in five of six cases. Material sensitivity
is itself material-dependent: Mooney-Rivlin is the most fragile of the
three materials on both geometries ($4.71\times$ on B1, $5.47\times$ on
B2), and Arruda-Boyce is consistently the most robust ($4.85\times$ on
B1, only $2.27\times$ on B2 -- the mildest degradation measured anywhere
in this study).

\subsection{Zero-shot resolution invariance}
\label{sec:resolution-invariance}
A single network trained jointly on two meshes (B1$\times$Neo-Hookean at
$N{=}21$ and $N{=}33$) is evaluated, with no retraining or fine-tuning,
directly on seven mesh resolutions never seen during training
($N=13,17,25,29,37,41,49$, two of them coarser than either training mesh
and five finer). Because the network was never shown these resolutions,
this tests genuine zero-shot generalization across discretization density,
which the Transolver architecture supports natively: it operates on the
nodes it is given, without a fixed input dimensionality tied to a
particular mesh. Each test resolution is compared against a common
fine-mesh finite-element reference ($N{=}101$, 10,201 nodes). Across all
three materials and all seven unseen resolutions the error stays between
$5.0\%$ and $10.6\%$, with no retraining anywhere -- that is the
resolution-invariance property claimed for operator-learning methods.

Reading down the columns rather than across them shows that zero-shot
transfer to meshes much finer than the training ones is not free: two of
the three materials reach their best accuracy near $N{=}21$--$33$ and then
get worse on the finest meshes (Neo-Hookean bottoms out at $N{=}29$ and
rises $28.7\%$ by $N{=}49$; Arruda-Boyce bottoms out at $N{=}37$ and rises
$11.6\%$), while Mooney-Rivlin alone keeps improving all the way to the
finest mesh tested. The three B2 cases needed a real fix before a valid
zero-shot result existed for them at all: the per-component validation
metric used for checkpoint selection divides each displacement component
by its own magnitude, and on B2's geometry the resulting quantity rises
over an interval where the field is in fact getting closer to the
reference, so early stopping fired within the first one or two validation
events on every B2 run and every subsequent measurement was made on models
trained for a few dozen epochs. Switching the selection criterion to the
combined norm $\|\mathbf{e}\|/\|\mathbf{u}\|$ and re-running fixed this:
B2$\times$Neo-Hookean's validation error fell from $0.9986$ to $0.0330$ on
the same per-component metric that had condemned it. With this fix, B2
does work -- $7.1\%$ to $26.9\%$ across seven unseen meshes, comparable to
B1 in the middle of the range but markedly worse at both ends, so its
error varies by a factor of $3.8$ across the sweep where the worst B1 case
varies by $2.1$: B2's resolution invariance is real and weaker than B1's.
One methodological point is worth stating plainly: the superseded
(uncorrected) B2 model's error was almost flat across the mesh, which
could easily be misread as the strongest resolution invariance in the
study -- it is the opposite. A model whose output barely responds to its
input produces nearly the same field, and therefore nearly the same
error, on every mesh; insensitivity and invariance are indistinguishable
in that column, and flatness of the error across resolutions is not, on
its own, evidence of the property this section is testing.

Placing the operator and the finite-element solver on a single
accuracy-cost plot (both methods scored against the same common $N{=}101$
reference, on the same 20 problem instances) settles that the two methods
do not compete on accuracy at all: the finite-element solver at its
coarsest setting ($N{=}13$, 169 nodes, 8.9~s) reaches $0.608\%$, already
$6.1\times$ more accurate than the operator at its best ($3.69\%$ at
$N{=}37$) -- there is no mesh in this sweep at which the operator matches
even the cheapest finite-element solve. The operator's case rests instead
on how cost scales: its inference cost is essentially flat in mesh size
($4.584$--$5.563$~ms from 169 to 2,401 nodes, a fourteenfold increase in
problem size for no measurable increase in cost), so the speed-up climbs
from $1{,}630\times$ to $25{,}676\times$ across the sweep -- that trend is
the substantive result, not a speed-up quoted at one resolution. This
flatness does not extrapolate indefinitely: measured directly at $N{=}1401$
($1{,}962{,}801$ nodes, far outside this sweep's own range), inference cost
rises to $2{,}286.7$~ms/sample, roughly $500\times$ the cost at the
smallest node count tested, though still $59\times$ faster than torch-fem's
own matched-precision solve at that same $N$ ($133.83$~s).

\subsection{Error in derived physical quantities}
Every operator error reported above is a displacement error -- the most
forgiving quantity that could be asked about: it is the primary unknown,
what the network is trained to produce, and smoother than its own
derivatives. Stress depends on the displacement gradient and so loses an
order of accuracy; reaction forces are an integral of that stress over the
supports. A $9\%$ displacement error therefore does not imply a $9\%$
stress error, and the size of that gap has to be measured, not inferred.
Each case is evaluated on 50 held-out samples (following the 800 used for
training) against the same checkpoints behind the results above, in FP64
(derived quantities are far more sensitive to round-off than displacement
is, although the network itself was trained in FP32); first
Piola--Kirchhoff stress is obtained as $\mathbf{P}=\partial\psi/\partial\mathbf{F}$
by automatic differentiation of the same strain-energy density the solver
and the training objective use.

The $H^1$ semi-norm behaves as theory predicts: on B1 it is roughly twice
the displacement error ($22.71$--$24.20\%$ against $10.34$--$11.71\%$),
the expected penalty for differentiating a field the network was never
asked to differentiate; on B2 the penalty is markedly smaller
($10.47$--$13.30\%$ against $7.21$--$10.47\%$). The aggregate stress error
(quadrature-weighted relative $L^2$ error of the full tensor in the
Frobenius norm) is $13.32$--$18.14\%$ across all six cases -- $1.3$ to
$2.0$ times the displacement error of the same case, and the number to
quote for stress accuracy; per-component stress errors are treacherous,
since a relative error divides by that component's own reference norm and
some components are near zero almost everywhere, while the Frobenius
column is unaffected by this small-denominator problem. Peak stress (the
maximum of $\|\mathbf{P}\|_F$ over the mesh) separates the two benchmarks
sharply: on B2 it is the most accurate quantity measured, $5.38$--$5.99\%$,
better than the displacement error itself; on B1 it is $19.87$--$47.53\%$
and highly variable from sample to sample, with the predicted peak
exceeding the reference peak in all three B1 cases -- no cause has been
isolated for this overshoot, and it is reported as measured. Reaction
forces are the best-behaved derived quantity measured: $4.66$--$12.61\%$
in the resultant and $7.10$--$12.86\%$ in the nodal distribution, the same
order as the displacement error and far better than the pointwise stress
it is assembled from, since the resultant sums Gauss-point stresses over
the whole support and the pointwise errors partially cancel. The three
field measures that involve a derivative of the displacement ($H^1$
semi-norm, tangent energy, aggregate stress) exceed the displacement
error in every one of the six cases without exception; the two quantities
that can beat it are the ones that are not pointwise fields -- the
reaction resultant and, on B2 specifically, the peak stress. A
displacement error quoted alone is therefore a lower bound on what a
downstream engineering quantity would see, not a reliable predictor of
it: across the quantities measured here that ratio spans $0.45\times$ to
$4.06\times$.

\subsection{Physics-informed vs. data-driven training, at matched cost}
\label{sec:pi-vs-dd}
Every operator above is trained by minimizing total potential energy, with
no reference solutions in the loss. The obvious question is whether that
principle earns its keep: the same architecture on the same mesh could
instead be fitted to finite-element displacements directly. Changing the
loss usually means changing the optimizer that was tuned for it, which
would confound the two effects, so the comparison was run as a
two-by-two: both losses under both recipes (Adam; AdamW with a one-cycle
schedule), all four at the same 75,000 optimizer steps, on
B1$\times$Neo-Hookean. Reading down the Adam column, the physics-informed
loss wins by $27\%$; reading down the AdamW+OneCycleLR column, the
data-driven loss wins by $34\%$. The ranking reverses, so neither training
principle is better than the other in any unconditional sense on this
problem -- a finding only the complete grid can support, since either
column alone would have licensed a confident and opposite conclusion. The
mechanism is that the optimizer does not treat the two losses alike:
switching from Adam to AdamW with a one-cycle schedule makes the
physics-informed model $30\%$ worse and the data-driven model $37\%$
better, so a learning-rate schedule tuned against one objective is not
neutral ground for the other.

One asymmetry survives the reversal and is not an accuracy figure: the
data-driven model needs a labelled dataset -- 800 finite-element solves,
$5.65$ CPU-hours at the measured cost -- before training can even begin,
a fixed cost the physics-informed model never pays, since its loss is the
energy of its own prediction. The data-driven model is more expensive by
this fixed amount ($18{,}924$~s under matched Adam, $18{,}694$~s under
matched AdamW+OneCycleLR) for every number of future inferences, not just
below some threshold: the two checkpoints' inference cost is effectively
identical ($4.625$ vs.\ $4.586$~ms/sample, a $0.8\%$ difference,
consistent with sharing the identical architecture), so the per-inference
term cancels and what remains is the label-generation cost, which
dominates by more than an order of magnitude in both pairings.

\subsection{Verification against manufactured solutions}
Every accuracy figure reported so far is measured against a finite-element
solution, which makes the finite-element method the one component of the
pipeline that is never itself graded: it defines the reference, so it
cannot be scored against it. A manufactured solution removes that
circularity by supplying an exact answer known in closed form. A
displacement field is chosen first and the governing equations are then
solved backwards for the body force that would produce it, vanishing on
the entire boundary so that homogeneous Dirichlet conditions are the
exact boundary condition and no solver change is required; the body force
is obtained by nested automatic differentiation of the strain-energy
density and checked against a central finite difference before any mesh
is built. Observed convergence rates for Q4 and Q9, fitted by least
squares on $\log h$, land on their theoretical values across all three
constitutive models -- a body force wrong by a sign or a factor would
make the discrete solution converge to the wrong function and these rates
would collapse. Because both orders are scored against the same exact
solution, they can be compared at equal cost rather than equal mesh
spacing: Q9 is more accurate at every matched degree-of-freedom count
($4.0\times$ at 162 DOF, $8.2\times$ at 578 DOF in $L^2$), and its margin
widens with refinement, exactly as its higher rate predicts.

A separate operator -- same architecture, same Q4 mesh, minimizing the
same discrete potential energy, scored by the same routine as the two
solvers, trained on a 64-member family of manufactured solutions with no
labels (the exact solution enters only the scoring, never the loss) --
makes the comparison three-way. The operator minimizes the same discrete
functional over the same Q4 space that the Q4 solver solves, so no field
it produces attains a lower discrete potential energy than Q4 does; but
that ceiling is a statement about $\Pi$, not about the $L^2$ error against
$u^\ast$, which is a different functional and does not inherit the
guarantee. At $N{=}17$ the operator is $2.42\times$ the Q4 error in
$L^2$, but the four norms do not agree on how far it is: in the $H^1$
semi-norm the ratio is $1.03\times$ and in stress $1.03\times$, both
effectively at the Q4 optimum, while $L^2$ sits at $2.42\times$ and energy
at $3.11\times$ -- an ordering inverted relative to the usual one, in
which $L^2$ is the forgiving norm and the derivative-based norms are the
strict ones. The inversion is consistent with what the training principle
optimizes: the energy functional is built from the deformation gradient,
so the strain and stress fields are the quantities the loss sees directly
and constrains hardest, while displacement is only pinned down through
them. For a physics-informed operator, an $L^2$ displacement error
overstates how wrong the mechanics are. The same derivation and mesh
sequence were repeated for Mooney-Rivlin and Arruda-Boyce; all rows land
on their theoretical rate, so the verification holds for every
constitutive model this study trains an operator on, not only the one
exercised above.

Two further operators, trained at $N{=}9$ and $N{=}33$ under the identical
protocol, give the operator its own convergence rate for the first time.
The operator does not converge: its $L^2$ error \emph{rises} with
refinement ($5.035\times10^{-3}$ at $N{=}9$ to $1.136\times10^{-2}$ at
$N{=}33$, a fitted rate of $-0.59$, where Q4 on the same three meshes is
$+1.99$); the $H^1$ semi-norm does improve, at $+0.78$ against Q4's
$+1.00$, so the picture is not that nothing improves, only that it does
so more slowly than the discretization it sits on. The explanation is
that the operator's error is dominated by optimization error, not
discretization error, and the two respond to refinement in opposite
ways: refining the mesh reduces the error that limits Q4 while leaving
the network's own optimization gap roughly where it was, on an enlarged
problem. Repeating the comparison on a 16-member family rather than a
single manufactured instance sharpens the same finding: the operator's
family-mean $L^2$ error rises $1.47\times$ from $N{=}9$ to $N{=}33$ while
Q4's falls $15.8\times$ over the same refinement (fitted rates: Q4
$1.99$, Q9 $3.01$, operator $-0.28$, negative on each refinement interval
separately), while $H^1$ and stress diverge far more mildly ($+0.74$ and
$+0.77$) -- the same ceiling-proximity effect visible in per-member
reliability as well as in the mean: the operator's coefficient of
variation across the family is two orders of magnitude larger than Q4's
in $L^2$ and energy, but close to Q4's own at the coarsest mesh in $H^1$
and stress.

\subsection{B2 accuracy regression: root cause and resolution}
\label{sec:discussion-b2}
B1 cases achieved significantly lower validation errors than B2 cases
under the same architecture, training protocol, and mesh resolution. This
gap was initially attributed to geometry -- B2's curved mesh and its
polar-to-Cartesian mapping -- but is instead traced to a specific, fixable
defect in B2's boundary-force training input, unrelated to the curved
geometry itself.

The per-node boundary force originally used for B2's external-work loss
term ($W$, in $\Pi=U-W$) was a raw pressure value times each inner node's
own exact radial direction, with no edge-length or quadrature weighting --
a convention carried over unchanged from B1's flat, straight-edge loaded
boundary. On B2's curved inner arc this is not the force the ground-truth
finite-element solve actually equilibrates against; the correct,
FEM-consistent nodal force is $13$--$16\times$ smaller in magnitude at the
study's standard mesh resolution. Substituting the correct, consistent
force alone made results markedly worse, not better: mean relative $L^2$
error rose from $32.46\%$ (the original, force-inconsistent baseline) to
$94.08\%$, close to the error of predicting no displacement at all. The
cause is that $\partial U/\partial u_v$ vanishes at the undeformed state to
leading order, so the external-work term's gradient is the only signal
pulling the network away from the trivial zero-displacement solution; the
smaller, correct force weakens exactly that signal, and training stalls
near the trivial solution instead of converging to the true equilibrium.

The fix normalizes the training objective's \emph{scale} rather than its
physics: each sample's $\Pi=U-W$ is divided by that sample's own mean
boundary-force magnitude, a fixed, network-output-independent positive
constant. Because $\arg\min_\theta[c\cdot f(\theta)] = \arg\min_\theta f(\theta)$
for any constant $c>0$, this leaves the true energy minimizer -- and
therefore the physics being fit -- completely unchanged; it only restores
a well-conditioned gradient magnitude for the optimizer. Applying this
corrected recipe reduced B2$\times$Neo-Hookean's validation error to
$9.11\%$, comparable to and in fact marginally better than
B1$\times$Neo-Hookean's own $9.59\%$, confirming that B2's high error was
not primarily geometry related -- though convergence was substantially
slower (best checkpoint at epoch 850 vs.\ 625 for the uncorrected recipe,
roughly $10\times$ the wall-clock time). The corrected recipe was then
applied to all three B2 materials: Mooney-Rivlin cleared a self-imposed
$9.00\%$ target on the first trial ($7.28\%$); Arruda-Boyce's first trial
reached $9.81\%$, narrowly missing the target, and an automatic search
over alternate recipes (higher learning rate, then a graded mesh) made
results markedly worse instead of better, so the first trial's $9.81\%$
was adopted as the final result rather than continuing to chase the
threshold.

\section{Results: B3 (3D Rocking Rubber-Mount Bushing)}
\label{sec:results-3d}

\subsection{Training and displacement accuracy}
A Deep Energy Method Transolver operator was trained for B3 at the fixed
production resolution (6,840 elements, 7,980 nodes) described in
Section~\ref{sec:discretization}. An initial training run, with the raw
$E,\nu,\phi$ values fed directly to the network, was unstable: the best
checkpoint reached only $26.2\%$ combined displacement error, with the
axial component $u_y$ (the smallest-magnitude component, driven by the
rocking rotation) at $93.6\%$ -- worse than an all-zero prediction. The root
cause was isolated rather than assumed: the raw inputs span three orders of
magnitude ($E\!\sim\!1000$, $\nu\!\sim\!0.45$, $\phi\!\sim\!0.05$), and a
controlled fixed-8-sample A/B test (identical seed, identical $4{,}000$
iterations, raw inputs versus standardized-to-mean-0/std-1 inputs) showed
combined error dropping from $10.7\%$ to $1.1\%$ and $u_y$ specifically
from $54.1\%$ to $4.0\%$ under normalization alone, with a visibly
smoother, monotonic loss curve replacing the earlier run's oscillation
between healthy and near-unit-error states. A full retrain with input
normalization enabled, on the genuinely disjoint held-out set, confirmed
this generalizes: combined displacement error $1.88\%$ ($u_x$ $1.94\%$,
$u_y$ $16.16\%$, $u_z$ $1.42\%$), in $6{,}980.3$~s of GPU wall-clock time.
$u_y$ remains the least accurate component, consistent with its smaller
magnitude contributing proportionally less to a pure energy-minimization
loss.

\subsection{Energy and reaction accuracy}
Total strain energy and the reaction moment about the rocking axis are
computed directly from a known displacement field via the same potential-energy
functional used in training, with no new finite-element solve (the reaction
at a constrained node is the derivative of total potential energy with
respect to displacement there, by the same variational argument any
finite-element solver uses internally). Both are well reproduced: mean
relative error $0.30\%$ for strain energy and $2.67\%$ for the reaction
moment.

\subsection{Regional Cauchy stress: metric methodology and local integration refinement}
\label{sec:b3-stress}
A naive relative error on the signed per-sample average of $\sigma_{xx}$
within a fixed region near the groove gave an unusable $1{,}683.6\%$ mean
error, traced to near-zero denominators when tension and compression
partially cancel within the region for a given sample. Replacing it with a
volume-weighted, quadrature-based full stress-\emph{tensor} relative field
error (which cannot collapse to a near-zero denominator) gave $71.8\%$ mean
/ $68.4\%$ median -- still far above a $5$--$10\%$ target, and computed from
only 6 Gauss-quadrature points in the fixed region at the production
resolution, below a reliable sample-count threshold.

Two numerical-integration refinements were then tested, independently of
the operator's own mesh discretization. On the \emph{evaluation} side
(no retraining): torch-fem's shape-function evaluator accepts arbitrary
local coordinates, so a finer internal Gauss--Legendre grid re-samples the
same, already-known coarse-element displacement field at up to $994$
region points (versus $6$ by default), with the $6{,}840$-element operator
discretization completely unchanged. The error stabilizes from $292$
region points onward (successive changes under $0.2\%$), settling at
$62.2$--$62.3\%$ -- a real, converged gap, not an under-sampling artifact.
This motivated a matching change on the \emph{training} side: the DEM
background-integration quadrature was locally refined to
$10\times10\times10$ points, for only the four elements (of $6{,}840$) that
intersect the fixed region, with the operator's mesh, node count, and
element count left otherwise unchanged. Retraining with this change for the
same iteration budget reduced the converged regional-stress error from
$62.2$--$62.3\%$ to $29.7$--$29.9\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:b3-stress}) -- roughly
halving it, and confirming that part of the original gap was attributable to
under-resolved background integration rather than the network's own
representational capacity.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The screening and early-stopping protocol reached the best validation
performance within $375$--$1{,}050$ epochs across all six benchmark cases
(Table~\ref{tab:final-results}), substantially reducing the training
budget compared with a naive fixed-epoch setting. Taken together with the
GPU-native FEM benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:gpu-fem}) and the training-cost
comparison (Table~\ref{tab:cost}), a consistent efficiency picture
emerges: a GPU-native but still-iterative FEM solver is $71.7$--$171.5\times$
faster than the CPU reference once well batched ($\mathrm{bs}{=}128$); the
trained operator's amortized training cost per sample ranges from about
$0.64\times$ (i.e.\ more expensive, for the corrected
B2$\times$Neo-Hookean recipe) to roughly $15\times$ cheaper than a single
CPU FEM solve, case-dependent; and its post-training inference cost is a
further two-to-three orders of magnitude cheaper still
($\sim$$5{,}387$--$12{,}575\times$ vs.\ CPU), because inference replaces an
iterative nonlinear solve with a single forward pass.

The out-of-distribution evaluation is the clearest evidence in this study
that the network has learned a fit specific to its training distribution
rather than the underlying physics in a way that extrapolates: B1's
accuracy degrades by a consistent $4$--$5\times$ under a combined
stiffness/load shift of only $2$--$2.5$ standard deviations; B2's smaller
measured degradation is best read considering its already much higher and
more variable in-distribution error, not as evidence that the curved
geometry generalizes better. The resolution-invariance study and the
reference-solver mesh-convergence study of Section~\ref{sec:discretization}
address complementary halves of the same underlying concern about the
fixed operating mesh used throughout this study: the finite-element
reference itself is already well converged there, so the ground truth used
throughout is trustworthy, while the operator trained and evaluated at
that same resolution sits within a band that is representative of a wide
range of nearby resolutions, rather than being a fortunate one-off result
at a single arbitrary mesh size. The zero-shot resolution-invariance study
and the $N{=}1401$ deployment-resolution finding together show a
complementary point beyond that: a single operator generalizes well across
unseen \emph{nearby} resolutions without retraining, but accuracy at a
resolution far outside the training range depends strongly on which
resolutions were seen during training, not merely on how many optimizer
steps were spent -- a multi-resolution training set that includes
resolutions closer to the deployment target outperforms both a
single-resolution-trained zero-shot model and, surprisingly, direct
training at the deployment resolution itself.

A preliminary comparison suggests this resolution-generalization behavior
is not specific to physics-informed training: a data-driven operator
trained on finite-element labels from a coarse mesh ($N{=}13$) is most
accurate at its own training resolution but degrades to $25.9\%$ at the
farthest unseen mesh tested, while one trained on a finer mesh ($N{=}33$)
trades some peak accuracy for materially better generalization to unseen
resolutions -- the same qualitative coarse-vs-fine training-resolution
trade-off observed for the physics-informed operator above, now
demonstrated for a supervised baseline as well.

The manufactured-solution verification (Section~\ref{sec:results-2d})
delivers an important, non-obvious qualification to every accuracy figure
reported: a trained operator's error, unlike a finite element's, does not
shrink with mesh refinement under a fixed training budget, because it is
dominated by optimization error on an increasingly large problem rather than
by discretization error. This is consistent with, and helps explain, the
broader pattern that every field-derivative quantity (stress, strain
energy, $H^1$ semi-norm) is harder for the operator than the displacement
field it is trained to minimize energy over.

B3's regional Cauchy-stress result (Section~\ref{sec:b3-stress}) extends
this picture to a genuinely 3D, geometrically localized quantity of
interest. Local integration refinement -- applied only to the handful of
elements touching a region that matters, independently of the operator's
own discretization -- recovers roughly half of the gap, directly confirming
that the region's background numerical integration, not only the network's
representational capacity, was a real contributor to the original error.
The remaining $29.8\%$ gap, still above a $5$--$10\%$ practical target, is
reported here as an open limitation rather than a resolved result. Several
concrete directions are identified for future work, none attempted in this
paper: a stress- or equilibrium-aware physics loss term, analogous to prior
energy-based treatments of contact and fracture~\cite{bai2025energy,goswami2020transfer,wang2026xdem};
a mixed displacement--stress formulation, following complementary-energy
approaches~\cite{abueidda2023enhanced,rao2021elastodynamics,abueidda2021meshless};
PINO-type residual enforcement or Newton-operator hybrids~\cite{taghikhani2025neuralnewton};
a local or hierarchical representation with additional capacity in
stress-critical regions, in the spirit of graph-neural-network and
geometry-conditioned approaches~\cite{he2023gnn,dean2026piginot}; data-driven
stress supervision; a hybrid physics-informed-plus-supervised objective; and,
more generally, further study of separating the physics-integration
resolution from the operator's own resolution, which this result already
shows to be a productive axis on its own. Goal-oriented error estimation in
the finite-element sense~\cite{prudhomme1999goal,becker2001optimal} -- which
localizes a global discretization error to a specific quantity of interest
via an adjoint problem -- offers a complementary, more principled framing of
"where does a trained operator's error in a specific local quantity actually
come from" than the ablation-style diagnosis used here, and is a natural
direction for relating the two fields more rigorously.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A physics-informed neural operator, trained by directly minimizing total
potential energy with no labeled displacement data, reproduces the
displacement field of six 2D hyperelastic benchmark cases (two geometries,
three constitutive models) to within $7$--$11\%$ relative error, at a
training cost below one native finite-element solve for five of six cases
and an inference cost three to four orders of magnitude below a native
solve thereafter. The same approach extends to a substantially more
demanding 3D benchmark -- a rocking rubber-mount bushing with a
stress-concentrating groove -- where it reproduces displacement ($1.9\%$),
total strain energy ($0.3\%$), and reaction moment ($2.7\%$) accurately,
and, judged against the cheapest finite-element mesh matching its own
accuracy, remains $589$--$800\times$ faster per sample across all studied
comparisons while repaying its training cost within a few thousand solves
at most.

Three findings qualify this result and point to concrete future work.
First, derived physical quantities (stress, energy norms, gradients) are
systematically harder to reproduce than displacement itself, and a trained
operator's error in these quantities does not shrink with mesh refinement
the way a finite element's does, since it is dominated by optimization
error rather than discretization error -- an operator's accuracy should
therefore be reported, and targeted, per quantity of interest rather than
assumed to transfer from displacement accuracy alone. Second,
generalization -- across both out-of-distribution material/loading shifts
and deployment resolutions far from the training range -- is not free:
a single network generalizes well across nearby, unseen resolutions, but
a deployment resolution far outside the training range requires a
training set whose resolutions already span toward it, not merely a larger
optimization budget. Third, a quantity of interest that is both
geometrically localized and derivative-based -- here, regional Cauchy
stress near a stress concentrator -- can suffer from a confounded,
separable deficiency: this paper shows that refining the DEM background
integration in the region of interest, independently of the operator's own
discretization, recovers roughly half of a large initial error, but closing
the remainder is left as an open problem with several concrete candidate
directions identified in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
